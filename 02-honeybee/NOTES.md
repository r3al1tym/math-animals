# Plate No. 2: the honeybee and its comb

Of all ways to divide the plane into cells of equal area, the regular hexagons use the least wall: Hales proved it in 1999. A regular n-gon of unit area has perimeter sqrt(4n·tan(π/n)), so a unit cell needs 4.559 of wall as a triangle, 4.000 as a square and 3.722 as a hexagon; every wall is shared by two cells, so only the ratios matter. The construction lies under the comb, on its own lattice, cell for cell, and shows across the comb's growing margin, a band that follows its left edge round from top to bottom; faint circles run on two or three cells past the comb's other edges, fading, so the rule visibly sits under the whole comb. The page lays the build out in space there. At the margin's edge every lattice point carries a compass circle 5.3 mm across (the width of a worker cell across its flats), the closest circles can pack: they cover π/(2√3) = 90.7 % of the plane, and the gap where three meet is shaded in grained graphite, bounded by the three circles' arcs, so the missing 9.3 % reads as grey specks. Over three columns the drawn outlines fill their corners to the hexagons through the circles' points of contact, which cover all of it. Then the bees' first cups stand round on the drawn hexagons (round mouths, thick walls, new wax all but white), straighten onto them column by column, and are drawn out to full depth and take their contents. The front is ragged, cell by cell. Under the drawn-out comb the hexagons are drawn too, and no pencil shows past the wax. The construction is drawn once into a layer with a time map, so each line draws itself at the pencil's pace, from the margin's edge in.

The comb grows on that drawing. Each cell is ray-cast per pixel as a prism of wax: a hexagonal lumen whose six corners each have their own fillet radius, walls under a 0.3 mm coping, the axis tilted 13° toward the comb's top, and a base of three rhombi meeting R/√2 below the shallow corners, which makes the rhombi's diagonals stand at √2 to 1 as on a rhombic dodecahedron (so an empty cell shows the Y of the three facets in the window light). Light reaches a point if a ray toward the window climbs out of the cell's mouth; honey is a dished, refracting surface 0.2 to 0.8 mm below the rim that mirrors the window and absorbs blue along its path; cappings, brood domes and packed pollen sit at their own depths. In the build, cells rise first as thick-walled round cups of young, translucent wax along a front from the comb's top (the knife-cut edge at the foot of the fragment). Then each triple junction relaxes in turn, so the walls straighten one by one onto the drawn hexagons, as Karihaloo, Zhang and Wang (2013) proposed for warm wax; Bauer and Bienefeld (2013) found the hexagons made directly, so the page and its note call the flow proposed. Then the cells are drawn out to 25 mm of comb and it takes its colour: honey fills, cappings close from the rim inward, the brood is sealed. The fragment lies as it hung, top bar up, so the cells tilt up toward the top bar and, under the window at the upper left, each reads as a well: the rim's shadow falls across the upper-left of its contents, the lower inner wall catches the light, and the open honey is a translucent amber, straw to dark from cell to cell, darker toward each cell's middle where it is thickest, filled to a different level in each cell. A glint shows only where a cell's honey surface actually faces the window (its tilt, its dish and how far it climbs the wall all vary), so most cells have none and the rest differ in place and size. The zones read as a beekeeper sees them, in arcs: sealed honey along the top rim, open honey, an arc of pollen (matte, packed, yellow, ochre, orange and brown) round the brood, and domed tan brood caps in the middle and below, each boundary decided cell by cell. The outline is cut along the cells' own walls, with some edge cells broken in half along a ragged line, and straight only where the knife went through the top row along the top bar; near the rim each cell is up to 3 % larger or smaller and a hair off the lattice. The comb sits on the card with its swept shadow (each part as tall as its cells, kept light because wax and honey pass some light), a faint near shade 2.5 mm out, a darkening all round its foot and a tight contact core along the side away from the window, which carries the seating. Three workers at true size (12.6 mm long, forewing 9.2 mm) are laid in last: one set out on an insect pin centred over its label, square and head up, 13 mm up its pin, with the pin's steel and black head above it and the pin head's shadow across its back, and on the card the pin's shadow and the bee's own shadow 51 px down-right; two lie on the comb with wings folded (one on the brood, one on the sealed honey, with pollen in its baskets). Late in the build, cells that have reached their final state are copied from the baked comb, so a seek renders only the cells still changing.

The trials along the foot are the same cells, each of one area, as the other two regular tilings, drawn by the same tiling routine that draws the comb's lattice and set at 1.5 times true size (the heading says so) so the shapes read. Each is lettered with the perimeter of a cell of unit area, computed by the page: triangles 4.559, squares 4.000, hexagons 3.722, ticked.
